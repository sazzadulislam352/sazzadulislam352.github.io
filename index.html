Biography

Sazzadul Islam is a data scientist and researcher based in Washington, DC. His work focuses on explainable, data-driven machine learning and big data analytics models that support risk assessment, compliance and decision-making in banking and healthcare. He earned his M.S. in Big Data Analytics from Bay Atlantic University, graduating Summa Cum Laude with a 3.94 GPA, and received the university's Presidential Award in 2024.

Before his graduate studies, he spent four years in corporate finance and accounting in Bangladesh, where he managed financial reporting, internal audits, budgeting and regulatory tax compliance. This practical grounding in how financial institutions measure and report risk informs the applied focus of his research.

He has authored or co-authored eight peer-reviewed journal articles, five as first or sole author, which together have been cited 170 times. He is preparing to pursue a Doctor of Computer Science. His doctoral research will advance trustworthy machine learning for regulated industries: models that are accurate, transparent and accountable enough to guide high-stakes decisions.

Research Statement

Banks, regulators and healthcare providers increasingly rely on automated models to make consequential decisions: whether a bank holds enough liquidity to survive a shock, whether a transaction is suspicious, whether a borrower is creditworthy, or how a clinician should act on patient data. These models are often accurate but opaque, and many are not built for the volume and speed of modern data.

My research addresses this gap. I study how machine learning, deep learning and big data analytics can be applied to these decisions while remaining interpretable and auditable, so that their outputs can be trusted by risk officers, compliance teams, examiners and clinicians. Methodologically, my articles combine predictive modeling (for example, LSTM forecasting benchmarked against linear models) with quantitative evaluation of the data-quality, governance and explainability conditions under which such models are adopted. My work spans three connected threads: (1) deep learning for financial stress testing and credit risk under regulatory frameworks such as Basel III; (2) machine learning for anti-money-laundering and anomaly detection in large financial data pipelines; and (3) real-time human-AI decision support and quality automation for US healthcare systems.

Looking ahead, I aim to develop explainable, regulation-aware learning systems that can be deployed in supervised industries, and to study how such systems can be evaluated for reliability, fairness and compliance before they are trusted with high-stakes decisions.

[J1]VaR and CVaR-based stress testing using deep learning for liquidity risk forecasting and banking stability assessment2024Review of Applied Science and TechnologyFirst author49 citations+
Research problem
Conventional liquidity ratios and linear models can understate tail risk, weakening early warning and stress testing for bank stability.
Objectives
Validate a pipeline that combines deep-learning liquidity-risk forecasting with VaR/CVaR scenario stress testing, and test how stressed tail risk and governance maturity relate to bank stability.
My contribution
First author of a two-author study (with R. Sultana); led the study.
Methods
LSTM forecasting benchmarked against linear regression; tiered scenario stress testing (baseline, mild, adverse, severe); VaR and CVaR at 95% and 99%; correlation and regression analysis; reliability testing.
Data & design
Quantitative, cross-sectional case design: 192 aligned observations from four case banks, plus a survey of liquidity-risk professionals (212 usable responses).
Evaluation metrics
RMSE, MAE, directional accuracy; correlation (r), regression (β, R²); Cronbach's α.
Key findings Demonstrated
  • LSTM outperformed the linear benchmark: RMSE 0.042 vs 0.061, MAE 0.031 vs 0.047, directional accuracy 67.5% vs 56.2%.
  • Under severe stress, CVaR0.99 rose 77.7% against 51.0% for VaR0.99, indicating tail thickening that VaR alone misses.
  • Stressed CVaR0.99 was negatively associated with stability (r = −0.58; β = −0.46, R² = .47); governance maturity was associated with lower stressed CVaR (β = −0.29).
Practical applications Potential
Early-warning liquidity monitoring; informing liquidity-buffer and funding-structure decisions; complementing scenario-based stress tests with tail-sensitive measures.
U.S. relevance Potential
Relates to the liquidity risk management and stress-testing practices used in U.S. bank supervision. Results come from a four-bank case study and have not been tested on U.S. supervisory data.

[J2]Explainable data analytics in financial decision systems: Enhancing transparency in big data–driven credit risk and loan approval models2023Int. Journal of Scientific Interdisciplinary ResearchSole author43 citations+
Research problem
Big-data credit-risk and loan-approval models are often opaque; it is unclear how explainability relates to conventional predictive performance.
Objectives
Evaluate explainability attributes (explanation stability, sparsity and governance traceability) alongside predictive-performance indicators in credit decision systems.
My contribution
Sole author: study design, data analysis, modeling and writing.
Methods
Cross-sectional model-comparison design; feature-contribution (explanation) analysis; engineered behavioral features; regression modeling; reliability testing.
Data & design
1,248 loan-application records from a retail lending context, including applicant demographic and financial profile variables and engineered behavioral features.
Evaluation metrics
Pseudo-R², regression β and p-values, score stability index, explanation sparsity, misclassification clustering, Cronbach's α.
Key findings Demonstrated
  • The top three drivers accounted for 64.8% of total contribution magnitude, with an average of 4.2 features per decision.
  • Explanation stability (β = 0.842) and governance traceability (β = 0.311) were significant predictors of approval outcomes (p < .001).
  • Adding explainability measures raised pseudo-R² from 0.241 to 0.293; higher explanation stability was associated with less misclassification clustering (β = −0.118).
Practical applications Potential
Explainability metrics for credit-model validation and documentation; consistent reason codes for lending decisions.
U.S. relevance Potential
Relates to U.S. expectations for model risk management and for explaining adverse credit decisions to applicants.

[J3]Advanced financial data analytics for anomaly detection and pattern discovery in large-scale financial data pipelines2022American Journal of Advanced Technology and Engineering SolutionsCo-author41 citations+
Research problem
Detecting anomalies and recurring patterns in large, distributed financial data pipelines, where data behavior and pipeline execution both affect detection.
Objectives
Examine how data behavior, temporal regimes and pipeline context shape anomaly-detection and pattern-discovery performance.
My contribution
Co-author (second of two, with T. B. Sohrab).
Methods
Robust multivariable regression; mixed-effects modeling; moderation analysis; cluster-stability assessment across resampled windows; sequential-pattern and network-structure analysis.
Data & design
52,480 event-level scored transactions and 9,600 entity-window observations, retained after screening for missing identifiers, reconciliation conflicts, duplicate events and incomplete pipeline logs.
Evaluation metrics
Anomaly scores and calibrated alert rates, ranking concentration, detection latency, cluster stability index, R², intraclass correlation (ICC).
Key findings Demonstrated
  • Anomaly intensity was highly concentrated: the top 5% of events held about 47% of the total.
  • Mean calibrated alert rate was 2.9%, mean detection latency 2.84 s, and average cluster stability 0.71.
  • The model explained R² = 0.54 of anomaly-score variance; drift effects were stronger in cross-border and high-risk channels.
Practical applications Potential
Alert prioritization, drift monitoring and design of transaction-monitoring pipelines.
U.S. relevance Potential
Relates to fraud and financial-crime monitoring by U.S. financial institutions.

First author of a four-author study.

Qualitative, exploratory design study: document analysis, semi-structured expert interviews, framework-mapping workshops, thematic coding and a cross-domain adaptation matrix mapping software-QA methods to clinical needs.

Practical applications Potential
A blueprint for prototyping clinician-reviewed AI decision support in emergency and critical care, and a basis for future pilot and A/B evaluation.

[J7]AI-driven credit scoring and default probability modeling for Basel III risk-weighted asset optimization in banking2026American Journal of Scholarly Research and InnovationSole author2 citations+
Research problem
AI credit scoring can improve probability-of-default (PD) estimation, but weak data quality, governance and explainability limit trust and its value for Basel III capital optimization.
Objectives
Measure two pathways: from AI credit-scoring capability to PD modeling effectiveness, and from PD effectiveness to Basel III risk-weighted-asset (RWA) optimization.
My contribution
Sole author: study design, survey instrument, statistical analysis and writing.
Methods
Quantitative, cross-sectional case study; reliability testing, Pearson correlation, two-stage multiple regression and moderation testing.
Data & design
212 valid responses (70.7% response rate) from a cloud-enabled enterprise case bank.
Evaluation metrics
Cronbach's α, Pearson r, R², standardized β, ΔR² for interaction effects.
Key findings Demonstrated
  • AI capability (β = 0.34), governance maturity (β = 0.25), data quality (β = 0.21) and explainability readiness (β = 0.14) jointly explained 57% of variance in PD effectiveness.
  • PD effectiveness was the strongest predictor of RWA optimization (β = 0.51; R² = 0.49).
  • Governance strengthened the effect of AI capability on PD outcomes (interaction β = 0.11, p = .031).
Practical applications Potential
Guiding investment in model governance and data quality alongside AI credit models.
U.S. relevance Potential
Relates to capital-adequacy requirements that U.S. banking regulators apply under the Basel III framework.

[J8]AI- and ML-driven predictive quality orchestration for US healthcare and HRM systems2025International Journal of Applied MathematicsCo-author1 citation+
Research problem
Software quality assurance in regulated, Agile DevOps settings needs better test intelligence, defect forecasting and compliance control.
Objectives
Develop and validate predictive models for quality orchestration, embed them in the DevOps toolchain, and compare outcomes in healthcare and HRM systems.
My contribution
Co-author (third of nine).
Methods
Random Forest, LSTM and Gradient Boosting Machine models; 80/20 train–test split with 10-fold cross-validation; feature selection with Recursive Feature Elimination and PCA; correlation, regression and structural equation modeling; multi-criteria decision analysis.
Data & design
Anonymized data from five U.S.-based healthcare and HRM technology organizations over a 36-month period: defect logs, test-execution reports, compliance-audit outcomes and CI/CD pipeline metrics, with structured interviews.
Evaluation metrics
Accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, ROC-AUC, MAE, RMSE; Cronbach's α and variance inflation factor (VIF) checks.
Key findings Demonstrated
  • Gradient Boosting performed best (accuracy 94.5%, precision 92.8%, recall 94.1%, F1 0.94, ROC-AUC 0.96), ahead of LSTM (93.8%) and Random Forest (91.4%).
  • Healthcare systems had lower defect density (3.85 vs 4.27 per KLOC), higher test coverage (89.5% vs 85.2%) and faster defect resolution (10.6 vs 13.2 hours) than HRM systems.
  • After implementation, the compliance deviation rate fell from 11.8% to 4.6% (a 61% reduction) and audit readiness improved by 25.9%.
Practical applications Potential
Defect forecasting and risk-based test prioritization in regulated software teams.
U.S. relevance Potential
Relates to the reliability and HIPAA-compliant operation of software used in U.S. healthcare.
